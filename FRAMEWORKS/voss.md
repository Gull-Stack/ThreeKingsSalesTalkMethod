# Chris Voss — Never Split the Difference

Former FBI lead international hostage negotiator. The method: get what you want by making the other side feel safe, heard, and in control.

## Core techniques

### Accusation audit
Name the worst thing they might think about you — out loud — before they do. Disarms the objection preemptively.
- "You're probably annoyed that a stranger is messaging you on a Thursday morning."
- "You've probably heard this pitch a hundred times from advisors who don't understand federal benefits."

### Mirroring
Repeat the last 1–3 words of what they said, as a question. Forces elaboration. Makes them feel heard.
- Them: "We've looked at this before and it didn't work out."
- You: "Didn't work out?"

### Labeling
Name the emotion you hear. Format: "It sounds like…" / "It seems like…" / "It feels like…" — never "I think you feel."
- "It sounds like you've been burned by advisors who didn't actually know the FERS system."
- "It seems like the real issue isn't budget, it's trust."

### No-oriented questions
People defend "no" more strongly than they claim "yes." Frame asks so the protective answer is "no."
- Not: "Do you have 15 minutes?"
- Instead: "Would it be a bad idea to take 15 minutes to see the actual numbers on your pension?"

### That's right
The magic moment. When the other side says "that's right" (not "you're right" — that's dismissal), you have summarized their world better than they could. Now they trust you.

### Calibrated questions
Open-ended, starting with "how" or "what." Never "why" (accusatory). Never yes/no (closes).
- "How am I supposed to do that?"
- "What about this is most important to you?"
- "How would you like me to proceed?"

## Voice

Late-night FM DJ voice. Slow. Calm. Low. Conveys absolute control without threat. Never raise it.

## When Voss wins alone

When the buyer is defensive, when trust is the gate, when the objection is emotional not logical. Voss earns the right to keep talking. Belfort and Miller deliver the payload once Voss has done that.
